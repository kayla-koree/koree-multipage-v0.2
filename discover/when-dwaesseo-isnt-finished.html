When “됐어 (dwaesseo)” isn't quite “It's done.”

One short word can close a task, close an offer, or close a conversation — and it doesn't announce which.

됐어 (dwaesseo) shows up early in most people's Korean, usually glossed as “It's fine” or “That's enough.” Both are reasonable. Neither one prepares you for how differently this word can land depending on where you catch it.

Most learners meet 되다 first, in its calm, textbook form — "to become," "to work out," "to turn out fine." 됐어 is technically just its past tense. Nothing about a conjugation chart prepares you for how sharp it can sound coming out of someone's mouth in the middle of an argument.

The version you already know

If you've watched any slice-of-life drama, there's a good chance you've already heard this one. Someone finishes a task — the dishes, packing a bag, fixing something — and a person nearby checks in.

A ROOMMATE CHECKS IN

룸메이트: 설거지 다 했어?
나: 어, 됐어.
“Did you finish the dishes?” — “Yeah, it's done.”

That's 됐어 behaving exactly like the dictionary promises. It comes from 되다, a verb that covers becoming, working out, and being enough — so on the surface, 됐어 is simply announcing that something reached the point of “enough.”

Then it shows up where nothing got finished

Now picture a different moment. A parent offers to send cab fare. A friend offers more coffee, a ride, a hand with something heavy.

A PARENT OFFERS TO HELP

엄마: 택시비 보내줄까?
나: 됐어, 엄마. 버스 타면 돼.
“Should I send you cab fare?” — “It's okay, mom. I'll just take the bus.”

No task got completed here. And yet 됐어 fits the moment perfectly — this time closer to “No, I'm good” than “It's done.” The word hasn't changed. The situation around it has.

What's actually closing

It helps to think of 됐어 as marking a stopping point rather than one fixed feeling. What stops depends on what came right before it — a task, so it reads as finished; an offer, so it reads as declined; sometimes, a conversation itself.

AN APOLOGY THAT'S BEEN HEARD BEFORE

동생: 나 또 늦을 것 같아, 미안...
나: 됐어. 이번엔 그냥 얘기하지 말자.
“I think I'm running late again, sorry...” — “Enough. Let's just not get into it this time.”

Here, 됐어 is closing the topic, not accepting the apology. Said with a small laugh, the same line can sound forgiving. Said flat and fast, it can sound like a door shutting. Tone is doing most of the work the dictionary can't.

Koree note: 됐어 isn't really naming an emotion. It's marking that something reached its stopping point — and what's stopping is the part worth listening for.

A word that assumes you're close

됐어 sits at the casual end of Korean speech — the level used with friends, siblings, or people close to you in age. Aimed at someone you'd normally speak more formally to, it can land as blunt even when nothing rude was intended.

DECLINING HELP, A LITTLE PROUD ABOUT IT

친구: 내가 도와줄까?
나: 됐어, 내가 알아서 할게.
“Want me to help?” — “No, I've got it myself.”

This last one adds a third color: independence, occasionally with a trace of pride, or mild irritation that help was offered at all. It's also one of the rare Korean expressions that seems to come with its own gesture — a small wave, a hand lifted, palm out. Even muted, that motion alone can sometimes tell you which 됐어 you're hearing before the sentence finishes.

There's also a doubled-up version worth knowing: 됐어, 됐어, said twice, tends to push further toward impatience — closer to "okay, okay, that's enough" than a single clean close. Repeating it rarely makes it softer.

The next time 됐어 turns up in something you're watching, try catching the half-second right before it plays. A dropped shoulder, a half-smile, a flat stare — that's usually where the real answer is sitting, not in the word itself.
